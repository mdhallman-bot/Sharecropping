Before you start

Back on Day 2, the Freedmen's Bureau material raised a promise that never fully happened: 40 acres and a mule — land of their own for formerly enslaved families. It never came. Here's what came instead.

85%
of Black farmers in Mississippi in 1900 did not own the land they farmed. (Mississippi Department of Archives and History.) Most were sharecroppers or tenants — the rate varied some state to state, but this was the rule across the South, not the exception.

Today you'll read the actual contract that governed a family in that 85%, build the numbers yourself to see exactly how the math worked against them, then read one person's own words about living it, and a poem that answers back.

Document 1: A Sharecropping Contract, 1882

Grimes Family Papers, North Carolina, 1882. Source: Digital Inquiry Group (inquirygroup.org).

Select any text, then click a color: green for something the sharecropper is promised or granted, red for something denied, restricted, or controlled by the landowner.

To every one applying to rent land, the following conditions must be read, and agreed to: For every 30 acres of land (rented by sharecroppers), I agree to furnish the mule team, plow, and farming implements. The sharecroppers are to have half of the cotton, corn, peas, pumpkins, and potatoes they grow if the following conditions are complied with, but—if not—they are to have only two-fifths.

For every mule or horse furnished by me there must be 1000 good sized rails (logs) hauled, and the fence repaired if I so direct. All sharecroppers must haul rails (logs) and work on the fence whenever I may order. The wood must be split and the fence repaired before corn is planted. No cotton must be planted by sharecroppers on their home patches of land.

No sharecropper is to work off the plantation when there is any work to be done on the land he has rented, or when his work is needed by me. Every sharecropper must be responsible for all farming gear placed in his hands, and if not returned must be paid for unless it is worn out by use.

Nothing can be sold from their (sharecroppers’) crops until my rent is all paid, and all amounts they owe me are paid in full. I am to gin & pack all of the cotton and charge every sharecropper an eighteenth of his part, the cropper to furnish his part of the bagging, ties, & twine. The sale of every sharecropper’s part of the cotton to be made by me when and where I choose to sell, and after deducting all they owe me.

Document 2: Build the Ledger

Use the contract's own terms above to work out what a real family would have taken home. Each field checks itself — try your own math first.

The situation:
  • A family rents 30 acres under this exact contract and hauls their rails/repairs their fence as required, so they qualify for the full half share (not two-fifths).
  • At harvest, their 30 acres produced 6 bales of cotton (a bale = 500 lbs).
  • Cotton was selling for about $45 per bale in 1882.
  • Per the contract, the landowner's gin charges 1/18 of the sharecropper's part for ginning and packing.
  • Over the year, the family bought food, cloth, and tool repairs on credit from the plantation store: $150 total.
Guiding question: This family worked all year and brought in a full harvest — no drought, no disaster. Why did they still end the year owing money? Use a specific number from your ledger.

Document 3: A Sharecropper's Own Words

James Lucas, born 1833, Adams County, Mississippi. Interviewed in the 1930s as part of the WPA Federal Writers' Project — a first-person account recorded decades after emancipation. Public domain (U.S. federal government work).

Document 4: "I, Too" — Langston Hughes (1926)

Public domain (published 1926). Hughes wrote this decades after Reconstruction, but the promise-vs-reality gap it describes is the same one this unit has been tracing since Day 2.

I, too, sing America.
I am the darker brother.
They send me to eat in the kitchen
When company comes,
But I laugh,
And eat well,
And grow strong.

Tomorrow,
I'll be at the table
When company comes.
Nobody'll dare
Say to me,
"Eat in the kitchen,"
Then.

Besides,
They'll see how beautiful I am
And be ashamed—

I, too, am America.
